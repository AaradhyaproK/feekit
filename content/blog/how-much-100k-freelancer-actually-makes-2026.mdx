## 1. The Self-Employment Tax (SECA) Shock: 15.3%

The single biggest tax trap for new contractors is the **Self-Employment Contributions Act (SECA) tax**.

When you work as a W-2 employee, your paycheck shows a 7.65% deduction for FICA (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare). What most workers never realize is that their employer is legally required to pay an identical **matching 7.65%** out of company funds.

As a 1099 freelancer, you are both parties. You must pay both halves:

```
SECA Rate = (6.2% × 2) + (1.45% × 2) = 12.4% + 2.9% = 15.3%
```

### How the IRS Calculates SECA Tax
The IRS gives you a small statutory deduction: SECA tax applies to **92.35%** of your net Schedule C profit (reflecting the employer half you pay).

```
Taxable SE Earnings = $100,000 × 0.9235 = $92,350
SECA Tax Due = $92,350 × 15.3% = $14,129.55
```

Before you have paid a single penny of regular federal income tax, and before your state government takes its share, **$14,129.55 is already owed to Uncle Sam**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> You are permitted an **above-the-line deduction** on Form 1040 for 50% of your self-employment tax ($7,064.78). While this reduces your adjusted gross income for federal income tax, it does **not** refund the SECA cash already paid.

## 2. Federal Income Tax Brackets (2026 Reality)

Once your self-employment tax is determined, your regular federal income tax is calculated on your Adjusted Gross Income (AGI).

For a single filer in 2026 with $100,000 in gross revenue and standard expenses:
* **Gross Revenue:** $100,000
* **Less Business Expenses:** -$3,200
* **Net Schedule C Profit:** $96,800
* **Less 50% SECA Deduction:** -$6,841
* **Less 2026 Standard Deduction:** -$15,000 (est.)

* **10% on first $11,925:** $1,192.50

* **22% from $48,476 to $74,959:** $5,826.48

## 4. The 4 Hidden Friction Costs Most Freelancers Ignore

Taxes are only half the drain. In traditional employment, companies provide a safety net of subsidized benefits. In the 1099 realm, you must purchase this safety net out of pocket with post-tax dollars:

### A. Health, Dental & Vision Insurance (~$550 – $750/month)
According to healthcare marketplace data, an individual silver plan with reasonable deductibles costs between **$450 and $650 per month**, plus $50–$100 for dental and vision. That is **$6,000 to $8,400 per year**. While self-employed health insurance premiums are tax-deductible, they represent a direct cash outflow.

### B. Payment Processing Deductions (2.9% + $0.30)
If you bill clients through credit cards, Stripe, or online invoicing software, payment gateways take roughly 3% off the top. On $100,000 in credit card invoices, **payment gateways deduct ~$2,900 to $3,200**. Check gateway fee breakdowns on our [Stripe Fee Calculator](/tools/stripe-fee-calculator/usa).

### C. The Professional Stack & Hardware (~$2,500 – $4,000/year)
Laptop amortisation, cloud hosting, Figma, GitHub, Adobe Creative Cloud, Notion, accounting software (QuickBooks/FreshBooks), professional liability insurance (E&O), and high-speed fiber internet easily consume **$250 to $350 per month**.

### D. The "Zero PTO" Reality (~$7,700 Hidden Cost)
If a W-2 employee takes 4 weeks of paid vacation, 10 federal holidays, and 5 sick days, their paycheck never blinks. If a freelancer takes 4 weeks off, **their billing drops to zero**. 

To take 4 weeks of vacation on a $100k target, you must earn $100,000 across **48 working weeks ($2,083/week)** instead of 52 weeks ($1,923/week). Failing to account for this leaves freelancers feeling perpetually trapped on a treadmill.

## 3 Legal Strategies to Rescue $12,000+ Every Year

Fortunately, the US tax code provides significant tax shelters designed specifically for business owners that W-2 employees cannot access. If you are billing $100,000, implementing these three strategies immediately will dramatically improve your net margin:

### Strategy 1: The S-Corp Election (Save $4,000 – $7,000 in SECA)
Once your net freelance profit consistently crosses **$80,000**, electing to have your LLC taxed as an **S-Corporation (IRS Form 2553)** becomes extremely lucrative.

Under an S-Corp, you split your $100,000 earnings into two buckets:
1. **W-2 Reasonable Salary (e.g., $55,000):** Subject to 15.3% payroll tax ($8,415).
2. **Shareholder Distribution (e.g., $45,000):** **100% exempt from the 15.3% self-employment tax.**

```
Annual Tax Savings = $45,000 × 15.3% = $6,885 Saved Per Year
```

Even after paying $1,500/year for payroll software (Gusto) and corporate tax filing, you pocket over **$5,300 in pure cash savings** every single year. Read our full [LLC vs S-Corp Tax Guide](/blog/llc-vs-scorp-freelance-tax-guide) for a detailed breakdown.

### Strategy 2: The Solo 401(k) / SEP IRA Pre-Tax Shield
Unlike employees capped at $23,500 in 401(k) contributions, solo business owners can contribute as **both employee and employer**.
* **As Employee:** Up to $23,500 in salary deferral.
* **As Employer:** Up to 25% of net adjusted business compensation.

By contributing $30,000 to a Solo 401(k), a $100k freelancer slashes their federal taxable income bracket, saving **$6,600+ in immediate federal income tax**.

### Strategy 3: Section 199A Qualified Business Income (QBI) Deduction
Under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, eligible pass-through business owners can deduct up to **20% of their net qualified business income** directly from their taxable income base. On an $80,000 qualified profit, this provides an automatic **$16,000 phantom deduction**, reducing your federal tax liability by up to $3,520.

## How to Price Yourself: The Billable Hours Reality

If $100,000 gross leaves you with only $55,000 net, what hourly rate should you actually charge?

Many beginners make the fatal mistake of dividing $100,000 by 2,080 hours (40 hours × 52 weeks = $48/hour). **This is financial suicide.**

In freelancing, you cannot bill 40 hours per week. Between client proposals, invoicing, code reviews, email communications, and administrative marketing, **only 55% to 65% of your hours are billable**.

```
Standard Full-Time Year:        2,080 hours
Less 4 Weeks Vacation/Sick:      -160 hours
Total Available Work Hours:     1,920 hours
Realistic Billable Ratio (60%): 1,152 billable hours/year
```

To gross $100,000, your hourly rate cannot be $48/hour:

```
Minimum Hourly Rate = $100,000 ÷ 1,150 billable hours = $86.95/hour
```

And if your goal is to **take home $100,000 clean in your pocket** after all taxes and expenses, your gross target must be ~$160,000:

```
True Target Hourly Rate = $160,000 ÷ 1,150 billable hours = $139.13/hour
```
